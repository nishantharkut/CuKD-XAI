ABSTRACT Wireless Sensor Networks (WSN) play a pivotal role in various domains, including monitoring,
security, and data transmission. However, their susceptibility to intrusions poses a significant challenge. This
paper proposes a novel Intrusion Detection System (IDS) leveraging Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) and
an ensemble machine learning approach combining Random Forest (RF), Decision Tree (DT), and K-Nearest
Neighbors (KNN) models to enhance the accuracy and reliability of intrusion detection in WSNs. The system
addresses key challenges such as the imbalanced nature of datasets and the evolving complexity of network
attacks. By incorporating Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique Tomek (SMOTE-Tomek) techniques
to balance the dataset and employing explainable AI methods such as Local Interpretable Model-agnostic
Explanations (LIME) and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), the proposed model achieves significant
improvements in detection accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score while providing clear, interpretable
results. Extensive experimentation on WSN-DS dataset demonstrates the system’s efficacy, achieving an
accuracy of 99.73%, with precision, recall, and F1 score values of 99.72% each, outperforming existing
approaches. This work offers a robust, scalable solution for securing WSNs, contributing to both academic
research and practical applications.

Wireless Sensor Networks (WSN) are a key component in
the field of wireless communication. These networks consist
of sensor nodes arranged in various topologies such as star,
tree, or mesh, which enable data sensing, transmission, and
flow within the network. The primary functions of these
sensor nodes include sensing, processing, computation, and

making it crucial to implement security measures to safeguard
the network and ensure its integrity [2].

An Intrusion Detection System (IDS) is a software or hard-
ware solution designed to detect and prevent unauthorized
access to computer systems or networks [4]. IDS can be
categorized into two types: host-based, which is integrated
into a host device to monitor processes and user activity
locally for intrusion detection, and network-based, which is
the more common type. Network-based IDS operates across
a network, monitoring traffic flow for potential intrusions
in a distributed manner [5]. IDS classifies the Aegean
Wi-Fi Intrusion Dataset (AWID) into four primary categories
based on network behavior: Normal, Flooding, Injection, and
Impersonation. Intrusion detection in IDS can be divided into
four methods:
Anomaly-based IDS, which is effective for detecting
unknown attacks by identifying abnormal behavior; Misuse-
based (or signature-based) IDS, which detects known attacks
by matching them with predefined signatures; Specification-
based IDS, which monitors network components like routing
tables, nodes, and protocols based on rules and thresholds
to identify irregularities; Hybrid-based IDS, which com-
bines anomaly, signature, and specification-based techniques.
Wireless networks have surpassed wired networks in terms
of flexibility, accessibility, mobility, and the lack of need
for additional infrastructure [6]. Wireless Intrusion Detec-
tion Systems (WIDS) are specialized sensors designed to
monitor and protect Wireless Local Area Networks (WLAN)
by continuously scanning all operating frequencies within
WLAN to detect and respond to intrusions [11]. The design
goals of WSN are centered on improving data quality,
reducing response time, enhancing network security, and
extending network lifetime. Anomaly detection is critical
for achieving these objectives, as abnormal data inevitably
arises due to various uncontrollable factors. Such data can
increase network traffic, delay, and waste limited energy
resources [10], while also compromising subsequent data
analysis and processing. Hence, timely and effective detec-
tion of anomalies is essential [7]. Despite the development
of some solutions, challenges remain in terms of detection
accuracy and speed. Leveraging artificial intelligence (AI)
presents a promising avenue for overcoming these technical
limitations. One significant challenge in WSN intrusion
detection is the imbalanced nature of datasets, where normal
instances vastly outnumber intrusion instances, negatively
affecting detection accuracy. Moreover, WSNs often consist
of a large number of sensor nodes, which leads to increased
computational complexity and scalability issues [8]. High
false positive rates and inadequate adaptability to evolving
attack techniques further complicate intrusion detection [9].
To address these challenges, we propose a model that
combines Machine Learning (ML) techniques with the
Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique and Tomek
Links (SMOTE-Tomek). This approach synthesizes minority
intrusion instances while removing Tomek links, thereby
balancing the dataset and improving detection accuracy and
reliability. Our model represents a significant improvement
over existing methods, providing an optimal solution for
WSN intrusion detection.
The primary contributions of this study are as follows:
1) Developed a cutting-edge Intrusion Detection System
(IDS) for Wireless Sensor Networks (WSN) that
leverages Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) for hy-

machine learning approach, significantly enhancing
detection accuracy, adaptability, and model perfor-

better accuracy and generalization.
2) Implemented the SMOTE-Tomek technique to over-
come the significant challenge of imbalanced datasets,
leading to improved detection of minority attack
classes in WSNs.
3) Incorporated state-of-the-art explainability tools such
as LIME(Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Expla-
nations) and SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)
to provide both local and global interpretations of the
model’s decisions, enhancing transparency and trust in
its predictions.
4) Developed an interactive Streamlit web application to
visualize the model’s predictions and demonstrate its
performance across different attack types and normal
conditions in WSNs, making the system accessible and
easy to interpret for users.
5) Utilized a robust ensemble of ML models includ-
ing Random Forest (RF), Decision Tree (DT), and
K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), with soft voting to
maximize the strength of multiple classifiers and
deliver more reliable intrusion detection.

III. METHODOLOGY
In this study, a comprehensive approach to intrusion detection
in WSN is presented, utilizing a robust ML model that has
been optimized for performance and interpretability through
advanced data processing and ensemble techniques. The
dataset, known as WSN-DS, contains diverse real-world
network scenarios and attack conditions. Preprocessing of
the dataset involved converting categorical variables into
numerical forms for compatibility with the machine learning
model. To address the class imbalance, SMOTE was applied
to balance the dataset. The data was split into 80% for training
and 20% for testing. Hyperparameter tuning was performed
using PSO to enhance model performance, as it proved to be
highly efficient and emerged as the most effective technique
compared to other optimization techniques. Various machine
learning methods were employed in the process of finding
best outcome, and at the end combining different algorithms
proved to be effective. Thus, an ensemble model comprising
RF, DT, and KNN algorithms was employed, with its
effectiveness evaluated using 5-fold cross-validation. Metrics
such as accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score were used to
assess the model’s performance. SHAP and LIME methods
were incorporated to provide global and local explanations
of the model’s behavior.
A. DATASET DESCRIPTION
A dataset called WSN-DS by Almomani et al. [41] is intended
for the identification of DoS assaults, comprising 374,661
records, with 9% identified as DoS attacks. The LEACH
system, a popular hierarchical routing system in Wireless
Sensor Networks (WSNs), was used to curate this dataset,
which covers typical network behaviors and four different
kinds of DoS assaults (Grayhole, Blackhole, TDMA, and
Flooding). Network Simulator 2 (NS-2) was used to gather
data, and after processing, 23 features were produced, adding
value to the dataset. Researchers looking to create strategies
for mitigating DoS attacks and improving network security
will find this dataset to be a useful resource [27].

1) MANUAL FEATURE SELECTION
Manual feature selection is the identification of features
for incorporation into a machine learning model based on
domain expertise, intuition, or exploratory data analysis,
rather than depending on automated techniques. This method
depends on human discernment to identify and eliminate
features considered unnecessary, redundant, or unhelpful for
augmenting the model’s prediction efficacy. In the WSN
dataset, the feature labeled ’ id’ was considered extraneous
for attack detection, as it failed to demonstrate any patterns
linked to assault scenarios. As a result, this feature was
eliminated during the preprocessing stage. Omitting this
feature improves the model’s effectiveness by decreasing the
amount of features it must analyze, enabling it to concentrate
exclusively on the most pertinent data.

C. DATA BALANCING
A dataset’s class distribution must be adjusted for data
balancing in order to guarantee that each class is represented
proportionately or evenly. This is especially crucial for
classification tasks because an imbalance in class can lead to
the model being biased in favor of the majority class, which
would make the minority class perform poorly in predictions.
The normal class, or scenarios without anomalies, has a far
larger number of records in the WSN dataset than do the
attack scenarios. The assault records form only 9% of the
overall dataset. When there is an imbalance, the model may
forecast classes inconsistently, doing well for the majority
class but poorly for the minority class. Consequently, if the
data continues to be uneven, performance measurements will
be less trustworthy.
The SMOTETomek methodology, a hybrid data balancing
method, is used to address the issue. To efficiently balance
the dataset, SMOTETomek combines Tomek Links and
SMOTE [33].
Using an oversampling approach called SMOTE, artificial
samples are produced for the minority class. SMOTE helps
to build a more broad decision boundary by creating new
samples by interpolating between existing ones rather than
replicating current minority samples [30], [31].
Tomek Links are groups of samples from various classes
that are closest to one another. These couples are frequently
found close to the class boundary and are regarded as
ambiguously classed. The dataset is cleaned by removing
these pairs, which also clarifies the decision border between
classes [32].
In concert, SMOTETomek detects and eliminates Tomek
linkages from the dataset after oversampling the minority
class. This procedure produces a cleaner dataset by removing
erratic and noisy records. By combining these methods, the
performance of the model is enhanced by the creation of a
more precise and comprehensive decision boundary.
Random Oversampling and Cluster-based Sampling ex-
hibit limitations that reduce their effectiveness compared
to SMOTE-Tomek. Random Oversampling often results in
the repetition of existing data, which does not contribute
new information and can increase the risk of overfitting.
Although Cluster-based Sampling offers an alternative,
it is computationally demanding and may not achieve the
same performance as SMOTE-Tomek. The SMOTE-Tomek
method combines the advantages of generating synthetic
samples and eliminating noisy instances through Tomek
Links, thereby producing a more balanced dataset with
clearer decision boundaries.
In summary, balancing the dataset is crucial for enhancing
classification performance in ML models. SMOTETomek
effectively addresses the imbalance issue, making the dataset
balanced and more suitable for ML algorithms.

2) PARTICLE SWARM OPTIMIZATION (PSO)
The practice of tweaking an ML model’s hyperparameters in
order to improve its performance is known as hyperparameter
tuning. The parameters that control the learning process are
known as hyperparameters, and they are established before
training. The accuracy of the model can be greatly increased
by carefully adjusting these hyperparameters. By determining
the ideal parameters, this procedure contributes to the model’s
increased robustness. The suggested approach makes use of
Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), an optimization method
inspired by nature, for this objective.
A computational method called PSO is used to improve
a candidate solution step-by-step based on a predetermined
quality metric. It mimics the social behavior of fish in a
school or birds in a flock to address optimization problems.
The suggested method begins with an optimizer choosing a
particle, which stands for a possible answer in the search
space. Next, these particles’ locations and velocities are set
to zero. After initialization, the global and personal best
positions are used to update the velocity and position. The
following equations can be used to update the position and
velocity:
Velocity update equation:
vi(t + 1)=vi(t)+c1.r1.(pbesti−xi(t))+c2.r2.(gbest−xi(t))
(3)
where, vi(t + 1) is the updated velocity of particle i at time
t + 1. vi(t) is the velocity of particle i at time t. c1 is the
cognitive acceleration coefficient. c2 is the social acceleration
coefficient. r1 and r2 are random numbers between 0 and 1.
pbesti is the personal best position of particle i. gbest is the
global best position. Position update equation:
xi(t + 1) = xi(t) + vi(t + 1)
(4)
where, xi(t +1) is the updated position of the particle i at time
t + 1. xi(t) is the position of the particle i at time t. vi(t + 1)
is the updated velocity of particle i at time t + 1.
Following
the

phase,
the

uses
cross-validated accuracy to assess each particle’s fitness,
which measures the performance of the hyperparameters
represented by the particle. Each particle in the swarm
corresponds to a specific set of hyperparameters. These
hyperparameters are evaluated by training model and
calculating its cross-validated accuracy to ensure the
solution’s robustness and generalization to unseen data.
Iteratively, the particles update their positions and velocities
based on their personal best positions and the global
best position of the swarm, ensuring a balance between
exploring new regions of the search space and exploiting
promising solutions. The personal best captures the particle’s
most successful hyperparameter configuration, while the
global best represents the highest-performing configuration
across the swarm. By leveraging this dual influence and
incorporating stochasticity to avoid local optima, the particles
systematically refine their positions. Through this iterative
process, the optimizer ensures an efficient search of the
hyperparameter space, ultimately converging on an optimal
set of parameters that enhance the model’s accuracy and
generalization.
In conclusion, PSO is a potent tool for fine-tuning
hyperparameters. Through simultaneous and global search
processes, it improves the effectiveness and efficiency of
the tuning process, making the optimization more adaptive.
As a result, the model performs better since the ideal
hyperparameters are systematically found, guaranteeing the
model’s accuracy and stability.
3) DECISION TREES (DT)
For problems involving regression and classification, a super-

according to feature values. Every leaf node represents a
class label or a continuous value, every branch reflects

4) RANDOM FOREST (RF)
One popular ensemble learning method for both classification
and regression applications is Random Forest. During the
training phase, this approach builds many decision trees,
and the final output is based on the mean forecast of each
individual tree or the mode of the classes. Random Forest
improves prediction accuracy and stability by combining the
predictions from several trees. The integration of randomness
in data sampling and feature selection contributes to the
construction of different trees, hence limiting the risk of
overfitting. It also offers estimates of feature relevance,
which helps with feature selection and can handle huge,
high-dimensional datasets. All things considered, Random
Forest is a strong and flexible ensemble technique that is
well-liked across a range of ML applications due to its
excellent accuracy and resilience.
5) K-NEAREST NEIGHBORS (KNN)
KNN is a straightforward, non-parametric, and lazy learning
method that predicts the result based on the k-nearest
data points in the feature space. It is utilized for both
regression and classification tasks. The algorithm runs by
storing all accessible data points and, for all new input,
calculating the distance to all training data points, sorting
these distances, selecting the k closest points, and utilizing
them for prediction. It uses neighborly majority voting for
categorization and neighbor average computation of values
for regression. KNN’s main benefits are its simplicity, ease
of use, adaptability, and short training period. Nevertheless,
it can be memory- and computationally-intensive, and it
is susceptible to data scaling and unimportant aspects. All
things considered, KNN is a useful technique for a variety of
applications, especially when the data quantity is reasonable
and the feature significance is obvious.
E. EXPLAINABLE AI (XAI)
The term Explainable AI (XAI) is a collection of approaches
and procedures designed to increase human comprehension
and transparency of artificial intelligence (AI) system out-
puts. Ensuring that users can comprehend and interpret AI
models, like ensemble approaches, is the aim of explainable
AI. Two frameworks have been used to explain the planned
task.
1) LOCAL INTERPRETABLE MODEL-AGNOSTIC
EXPLANATIONS(LIME)
The method known as LIME (Local Interpretable Model-
agnostic Explanations) is used to analyze and comprehend
the predictions made by intricate ML models. By using an
interpretable model to approximate it locally, it provides an
explanation for any black-box model’s predictions. LIME
offers many explainable models, each specific to each input
point, that closely mimic the behavior of the original model.
The explainable model guarantees that its predictions will be
quite close to the black-box model around this input point
[36]. LIME is used in this proposed effort to better understand
the behavior of the model and to determine the rationale
behind particular predictions.
2) SHAPLEY ADDITIVE EXPLANATIONS(SHAP)
The SHAP framework clarifies how various features con-
tribute to a model’s output, making it possible to evaluate
predictions made by ML models. SHAP values, which have
their roots in cooperative game theory, offer a way to
comprehend how each attribute affects the prediction for a
certain instance [34], [35]. It can be difficult to describe a
model’s reliability when it is complex. Although accuracy is
a useful performance metric, it doesn’t explain how the model
learns or generates predictions. In order to solve this, SHAP
provides visualization tools that illustrate how each feature
in a dataset contributes to the prediction, explaining the
results of any ML method. There are several SHAP explainers
available; the explainers used in this proposed work are Tree
and Kernel. The results of this proposed study are explained
via a series of plots provided by SHAP, which allow for an
in-depth examination of the properties of the model.
F. STREAMLIT
Streamlit is an application framework that makes it simple
to create online applications for ML. It has excellent com-
patibility with the main Python packages that are frequently
used for data processing and visualization in ML. Because
of this interoperability, developers may easily create visually
appealing and interactive applications to present their ML
models and outcomes [37].

5) SHAP STACKED FORCE PLOT OF THE TEST SET (GLOBAL)
Supervised clustering of force plots, also known as stacked
force plots, is a technique for understanding how various
characteristics affect a model’s predictions and for visualizing
patterns and trends in the model’s output. The vertical axis of
this kind of plot shows the cumulative effect of characteristics
on the decisions made by the model, while the horizontal
axis represents the test samples. This kind of plot is a two-
dimensional representation. You may detect trends and the
relative importance of factors in influencing the model’s
results by stacking the force plots to see how specific features
consistently affect predictions across numerous samples.
Figure 16 illustrates the output probability versus the
test samples for 200 test instances. The test samples are
represented by the horizontal axis, and the output probability
is displayed by the vertical axis. From the plot, a clear
dominance of the feature labeled ‘‘ADV_S’’ is evident.
Initially, this feature had a slightly negative influence on the
output, but as the samples progress, the feature increasingly
shifts towards a significant positive influence, strongly
affecting the model’s final predictions.
The effects of the feature named ‘‘ADV_S’’ can be
explicitly understood from Figure 17. The plot shows that
while the feature had a maximum negative impact on the
output, its impact was consistent throughout the test samples.
This consistency highlights the importance of ‘‘ADV_S’’ as
a critical feature for the model’s prediction capabilities, even
though its influence was primarily negative.
The results from both LIME and SHAP demonstrate a clear

across various cases. This consistency across both methods
enhances the reliability of the model’s explanations, as the
independent validation from each technique corroborates

V. CONCLUSION
This research presents a highly effective and scalable IDS for
WSN, combining cutting-edge ML techniques and optimiza-
tion strategies. The proposed system, leveraging PSO and an
ensemble of Random Forest, Decision Tree, and K-Nearest
Neighbors models, achieved an exceptional accuracy of
99.73%, with precision, recall, and F1-score all reaching
99.72%. The use of advanced data balancing methods like
SMOTE-Tomek significantly improved the detection of mi-
nority attack instances, overcoming the challenges posed by
dataset imbalance. Additionally, explainable AI techniques
such as SHAP and LIME provided crucial insights into the
model’s decision-making process, enhancing transparency
and interpretability, key for practical applications. Moreover,
the integration of the Streamlit web application allowed
for an intuitive and interactive interface, enabling real-time
validation of the model’s predictions across different attack
types and normal conditions, further demonstrating the
model’s robustness and user accessibility.Future work should
focus on optimizing the system’s computational efficiency
for deployment in resource-constrained environments and
extending its applicability to broader, more diverse datasets
to ensure greater generalizability across different WSN
scenarios.
